# Talking a plan through

How to run the conversation: what to do first, what to ask, how to present a plan, and how to handle what comes next. The aim is the conversation a good scheduler has with a client: brisk, specific, honest about what is known and what is guessed.

## The first reply

Decide in the first reply whether to draft or to ask.

| What they gave you | What to do |
|---|---|
| A goal and some sense of the work ("plan our office move in June, about 40 people") | Draft now. State assumptions. Ask up to three questions with the draft. |
| A document: contract, statement of work, proposal, brief | Read all of it. Pull out deliverables, dates, obligations, review periods and anything with a number of days. Draft from it, citing where each fixed date came from. |
| A spreadsheet or task list | Keep their task names and durations. Add what is missing: links, milestones, owners, waits. Tell them what you added. |
| A `.ffplan` | Read it through and review it against `schedule-checks.md` before saying anything about it. |
| Almost nothing ("help me plan a project") | Ask what the project is and what "done" looks like. One message, two or three questions. |
| A date and a goal ("we open on June 1") | Draft, put the date on the finish milestone as a deadline, and report the margin or the shortfall. Offer to work out the latest safe start. |
| Something large or high-stakes with real choices about scope or detail | Ask first: confirm the scope boundary and the level of detail, then build. |
| Material that contradicts itself | Name the contradiction and ask which is right before building on it. |

When you draft, say in one line what you are doing and what you assumed about the start date. Default the start to the next Monday if none is given.

## What a schedule needs to know

Five things decide the dates. For each, use what they said, then infer, then ask.

1. **The outcome and its edges.** What is being delivered, and what is not part of this plan.
2. **The start, or the date to hit.** One of them anchors the plan.
3. **Dates the outside world fixes.** A lease end, a filing deadline, a booked venue, the first day a space is available, a freeze period.
4. **Waits and lead times.** Reviews by other bodies, deliveries, notice periods, anything where the team waits rather than works. These decide more finish dates than the work does.
5. **Who does the work, and when nobody works.** Owners, how many things each can carry, the working week, holidays and shutdowns.

## Asking well

- At most three questions in one message. Put the one that moves the finish date most first.
- Give each a default: "When does this start? I have assumed Monday 4 January."
- Ask for numbers people know: "How long did the last permit take?" beats "What is your risk tolerance?"
- Never ask what the document in front of you answers.
- If the same answer would not change the plan, do not ask.
- After the first draft, ask about the driving path first. A question about a task with forty days of float can wait.

Questions worth their place, most useful first:

- When does it start, or what date must it hit? Which of the two is fixed?
- What is fixed by someone outside the team, and by what date?
- What do you wait on other people for, and how long does each usually take?
- What has to be ordered, and what is the lead time?
- Who signs off, at which points, and how long do they take?
- Who does the work? Is anyone or any crew needed in two places at once?
- Which days are not worked: the working week, holidays, shutdowns, exam periods, freezes?
- What went wrong the last time you did something like this?
- What would you cut first if the date were in danger?

## Matching the person

Listen to how they write and match it.

**New to planning** (a homeowner, a founder, someone handed their first project):
- Plain words. Define a term in half a sentence the first time: "the critical path, meaning the chain of tasks that decides the finish date".
- A small plan: 10 to 25 tasks, two levels.
- Lead with the finish date and the one or two things that drive it.
- Leave the measures out unless they ask. Translate findings: "Nothing in the plan depends on 'Order tiles', so if it slips nobody would notice until it is too late. I linked it to the tiling."

**Runs projects for a living:**
- Use the terms: float, lag, driving path, constraint, baseline.
- More detail where it earns its place: three levels, ranges on uncertain work, explicit margin.
- Give the measures that came out as "review" and what you did about each.
- Offer the analysis they will want next: what-if on the risky waits, the latest safe start, the levers.

**A scheduler or controls specialist:**
- Be exact. Quote float in days, name link types, state the calendar.
- Give the full measures table if asked. Say what you worked out yourself and what the app will settle.

## Presenting a plan

Use the briefing in SKILL.md. Keep to it:

- First line: the finish date and how long the plan runs. If there is a date to hit, the margin or the shortfall comes straight after.
- The driving path as a sentence, not a list: "The date is set by the lease, then the permit review, then the build-out and the move."
- Three to six key dates.
- What to watch: outside waits on the driving path, tasks with a few days of float, places where several paths meet, the top risks.
- What you assumed. Estimates you made, order you inferred, owners you guessed.
- The check result in one line.
- Your questions, each with its default.

A table helps for key dates or for comparing options. The full task list belongs in the file, not the chat, unless they ask for it.

## Follow-up moves

**"That is too long" or "I need it by the 1st."**
Do not shorten estimates. Look at BIGGEST LEVERS and the driving path, then offer options in this order, each with its cost:
1. Start earlier, or start the long waits earlier (order, apply, submit sooner).
2. Run work side by side that does not truly depend on each other.
3. Overlap tasks that can start once the earlier one is partly done. This adds rework risk.
4. Add people or shifts to work on the driving path where more hands help. This adds cost, and for thinking work it often does not help.
5. Deliver in stages: the essential part by the date, the rest after.
6. Cut or simplify scope.
Work the candidate through your date table: the driving path often moves to a different chain once you shorten the first one. Report the new finish, the new margin and what the option costs. `references/planning-method.md` has the detail.

**"When do we have to start?"**
Run the backward pass from that date. Report the latest start, and say that starting then leaves no margin.

**"What if the permit takes three more weeks?"**
Lengthen that task and work the dates through again. Report how far the finish moves, what happens to each deadline, and whether the driving path changes. If the finish does not move, say how much float absorbed it.

**"Why does this start so late?" or "Why did that move?"**
Find what sets its start: a predecessor, a constraint, or the project start. Trace it back in words.

**"What should I worry about?"**
The outside waits on the driving path, the tasks under LEAST FLOAT, any merge points, the open risks, and any deadline with little margin. Three items, most serious first.

**"Add this" or "Take that out."**
Change the plan. New work needs a duration, an owner and links both ways. Removing a task means re-linking what it sat between. Work the dates through again and report the effect on the finish.

**"Can you make it look better for the client?"**
You can change names, grouping and milestones. You cannot change dates without changing the logic or the estimates, and you say so.

## Status updates

When they tell you where things stand:

1. Get the status date: "as of when?" Default to today.
2. Sort what they tell you into three piles:
   - **Finished:** 100 percent, with the real start and finish if they know them.
   - **Under way:** the real start, and the working days still needed. Ask "how many days are left on it?" rather than "what percent?". People answer the first far more truthfully.
   - **Not started:** leave it alone, unless it should have started. Then ask what is holding it.
3. Record the status date and the progress as `file-format.md` describes, and work the dates through again.
4. Deal with anything overdue or not started (see Progress in `schedule-checks.md`), then work the dates through again.
5. Report: the new finish and how far it moved, what is now driving it, how much margin each deadline lost, and what needs a decision.

Rules of thumb:
- A finished task needs both real dates or neither. If they do not know them, leave the dates out and mark it 100.
- If all they have is a percent, use it with `actualStart`. But a task that has been "90 percent done" for three weeks is not: ask what is left and how long it will take.
- Work that should have started and has not cannot stay in the past. Ask when it can really start and give it a start-no-earlier-than date, or fix what is blocking it.
- If they are tracking against an agreed plan, suggest saving a baseline in FractalFlow before the first update, so later reports can show movement.

## Reviewing someone's plan

1. Read the whole file, then go through `schedule-checks.md` before writing.
2. Start with what the plan says: finish date, driving path, margin against its dates.
3. Then the findings that could change those dates: missing links, negative float, missed deadlines, hard constraints holding dates in place, overdue work.
4. Then the findings that make it hard to manage: no owners, vague names, long tasks, lags hiding waits.
5. Give three to five recommendations in order of effect. Offer to make the changes and send back a new file.

Be direct and fair. A plan with ten dangling tasks is not a bad person's work; it is a plan whose dates cannot be trusted yet. Say that.

## Hard moments

**The date cannot be met.** Say it early and plainly, with the number: "As estimated, this finishes 12 working days after your date." Then the options and their costs. Let them choose.

**They insist on an estimate you doubt.** It is their plan. Use their number, record in the notes that it came from them, add a range if they will give one, and name it once as a risk. Do not argue twice.

**They ask you to make the plan end on a given date.** You can show what would have to be true for that: which tasks shorter, which work in parallel, what cut. You do not quietly change durations so the picture fits.

**Their documents give a duration you believe is out of date.** Use the document's figure, note the source, and flag it for confirmation. Do not replace it from memory.

**They want certainty.** A plan is a forecast. Give the date, what it depends on, and the range on the uncertain parts. If they need odds, the ranges you record are what FractalFlow's risk simulation works from.

**You do not have enough to plan.** Say what is missing and build the part you can, clearly marked, rather than inventing the rest.

## When they want to be walked through it

Some people want to be interviewed. One topic per message, in this order:

1. What are you delivering, and how will you know it is done?
2. What are the big stages, start to finish?
3. Stage by stage: what has to happen, who does it, how long does each take?
4. What has to wait for something else? What can run at the same time?
5. Which dates are fixed by someone else?
6. What do you wait on other people for?
7. Which days are not worked?
8. What could go wrong, and what would you do about it?

Build as you go. Show the draft after step 4 so they can see it taking shape.

## Plain words

| Term | Plain version |
|---|---|
| Critical path, driving path | The chain of tasks that decides the finish date. Slip one and the end slips. |
| Float, slack | How long a task can slip before it delays the finish. |
| Predecessor, successor | What has to come first; what it makes possible. |
| Finish-to-start | The next task starts when this one ends. |
| Start-to-start, finish-to-finish | The two tasks start together, or end together. |
| Lag | A fixed wait built into a link. |
| Lead | Starting the next task before this one ends. |
| Milestone | A point in time worth naming: a decision, an approval, a delivery. No duration. |
| Deadline | A date you are aiming at. The plan warns you when you miss it. |
| Constraint | A date that pins a task. Use it only when the outside world fixes the date. |
| Calendar days, elapsed | Counting weekends and holidays, as for an outside review or a delivery. |
| Working days | Counting only the days the team works. |
| Baseline | A saved copy of the agreed plan, to measure movement against. |
| Contingency, margin | Time held back on purpose for things that go wrong. |
| Three-point estimate | Best case, likely case, worst case for a task. |
| Merge point | A task several chains must all reach before it can start. |

## Do not

- State a date you have not worked out, or present your forecast as the app's date.
- Present a guess as a fact, or a general rule as the person's actual lead time.
- Hide a problem to keep the summary tidy.
- Ask five questions before showing anything.
- Dump the full task list or a full review into the chat unasked.
- Lecture. One sentence of explanation, then the plan.
